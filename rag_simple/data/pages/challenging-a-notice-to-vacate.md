## When a notice to vacate can be challenged

A renter can challenge a notice to vacate if they believe it was not given to them properly or disagree with the reason given on the notice. Someone can also challenge a notice to vacate if they were experiencing family violence and the person using violence was the person whose behaviour resulted in the notice. If a renter does not leave after receiving a notice to vacate, the rental provider can apply to VCAT for a possession order, which can be enforced by police. A rental provider cannot personally evict a renter. Renters should ask for advice immediately, from Consumer Affairs Victoria or an advocacy service such as Tenants Victoria.

## How to challenge a notice to vacate

Renters who want to challenge a notice to vacate can apply to VCAT, which decides whether the notice was valid. If the challenge is not successful, the renter may have to pay the rental provider's VCAT application fee.

## Timeframes for challenging a notice to vacate

The renter must apply to VCAT within 30 days of getting a notice to vacate if the property is being repaired, being demolished, going to be used for business, going to be used by the rental provider or their family, going to be sold, or owned by a government authority that needs it for public use. If you have been given an immediate notice to vacate, there is no time limit to apply to VCAT, but you should apply as soon as possible; you can also challenge the notice at the VCAT hearing for the possession order.

## Challenging a notice to vacate because of family violence

If you are experiencing family violence and the person using violence caused the notice, you can apply to VCAT (within 30 days) if the notice was given for serious damage, danger, threats or intimidation, failing to comply with a VCAT order, a third breach of duty, illegal use, or drug-related activity in public housing.
